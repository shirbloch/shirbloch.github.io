About

I am a PhD candidate in economics at Georgetown University, where my research examines tradeoffs between representation and efficiency in democratic institutions. I apply tools from industrial organization to questions in political economy and public economics. My academic background spans applied mathematical economics, political science, and philosophy, a combination that continues to shape the questions I pursue. I am currently a program assistant at the DC Political Economy Center. Beginning this summer, I will also join the Georgetown Center for Business and Public Policy as a research assistant.

Selected Works in Progress

The Market for Representation: Campaign Finance, Party Entry, and Representation Quality
How do electoral institutions shape the supply of political representation, and at what cost to voters? I study Brazil's Constitutional Amendment 97/2017, which introduced a rising national vote-share threshold gating parties' access to public campaign funding, over a period in which the effective number of parties more than doubled before falling again. I recover a one-dimensional ideology score for each candidate from individual campaign donations, and estimate a nested logit demand system on 3.4 million candidate × municipality observations from the 2010 through 2022 Chamber elections, in which voters choose a party and then a candidate within its list. On the supply side, I model party entry as a free-entry game in which the threshold shifts entry costs and its predetermined schedule implements the variation that identifies them. Together, the two sides allow counterfactuals over threshold levels and strength, weighing reduced fragmentation and fiscal savings against a narrower ideological menu.
Heterogeneity in the Rise and Repercussions of Populist Rule
Tax Incentives for Electric Vehicle Charging Stations in Low-Income Communities
with Arik Levinson
From January 1, 2023 until June 30, 2026, the U.S. Inflation Reduction Act eliminated existing tax credits for building commercial electric vehicle charging ports in about one-third of the nation's 84,000 census tracts, and substantially increased the credit's generosity in the other two-thirds. To be eligible, census tracts needed to contain at least 10 percent nonurban census blocks, have poverty rates of at least 20 percent, or have median family incomes below 80 percent of that of the surrounding metropolitan area or state. We find no evidence that the new tax credits accelerated charging port construction in census tracts containing slightly more than 10 percent nonurban census blocks, with poverty rates slightly higher than 20 percent, or having median family incomes slightly below 80 percent of their state or metropolitan area. Nor do we find evidence that charging port construction differed on either side of geographic borders between eligible and ineligible census tracts.
Public R&D and Innovation: Evidence from Sputnik
with Walter Melnik and Andrew Smyth  ·  SSRN
This paper examines the innovation effects of the public R&D increase induced by Sputnik. We first document that this surprise R&D boon disproportionately affected missiles. Next, we estimate the causal effects of the shock for defense-funded and privately-funded missile patenting. Both effects are large, with the privately-funded effect more than twice as large as the defense-funded effect. The data indicate no major shifts in the quality of either type of missile patent. Our estimates suggest that the defense-funded effect was driven by de novo firms in novel locations, while the opposite was true for privately-funded patenting. Finally, citations made to missile patents from other technologies did not disproportionately increase, but we document innovation spillovers to non-missile patenting. In particular, over a dozen diverse technologies experienced large patenting gains in Sputnik's immediate aftermath. Though very costly in per-patent terms, these spillovers are compelling because missiles are a "lower bound case" for public R&D spillovers.
